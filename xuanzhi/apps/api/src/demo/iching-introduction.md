# I Ching (易经, Yìjīng) — Introduction

> Demonstration reference content prepared for 玄知 AI. It summarises widely accepted scholarship and does not replace the primary text or a qualified teacher.

## What the I Ching is

The *I Ching* or *Zhouyi* (周易, "Changes of Zhou") is one of the oldest Chinese classics. Its core consists of sixty-four hexagrams (卦, guà), each made of six lines (爻, yáo) that are either broken (yin, 阴) or unbroken (yang, 阳). Each hexagram has a name, a brief judgment (卦辞) and six line statements (爻辞). Traditional accounts attribute the trigrams to the legendary Fu Xi (伏羲), the hexagram judgments to King Wen of Zhou (周文王) and the line statements to the Duke of Zhou (周公). Historians treat these attributions as tradition rather than documented authorship; the received text took shape during the Western Zhou and was later expanded by the "Ten Wings" (十翼) commentaries associated with the Confucian school.

## Change, timing and conduct

The book's central idea is that all situations change, and that wise action depends on recognising where one stands in a cycle of change. The text repeatedly stresses timing (时, shí), position (位, wèi) and the appropriateness of conduct. A famous summary from the Great Commentary (系辞) states: 「一阴一阳之谓道」 ("One yin, one yang: this is called the Dao"), meaning that reality is an alternation of complementary forces rather than a static state.

The I Ching does not give simple "yes" or "no" answers. A reading describes a configuration of forces, the tendency of the moment, and the attitude that fits it. Classical readers used it as a mirror for reflection on ethics and decision making, not only for prediction.

## The eight trigrams (八卦)

Each hexagram is read as two three-line trigrams (卦). The eight trigrams and their traditional associations are:

| Trigram | Name | Image | Attribute | Family role |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| ☰ | 乾 Qián | Heaven | creative, strong | father |
| ☷ | 坤 Kūn | Earth | receptive, devoted | mother |
| ☳ | 震 Zhèn | Thunder | arousing, movement | eldest son |
| ☵ | 坎 Kǎn | Water | danger, depth | middle son |
| ☶ | 艮 Gèn | Mountain | stillness, keeping still | youngest son |
| ☴ | 巽 Xùn | Wind/Wood | gentle, penetrating | eldest daughter |
| ☲ | 离 Lí | Fire | clinging, clarity | middle daughter |
| ☱ | 兑 Duì | Lake | joyous, open | youngest daughter |

Two arrangements exist: the Earlier Heaven (先天八卦) sequence attributed to Fu Xi, which shows complementary pairs in balance, and the Later Heaven (后天八卦) sequence attributed to King Wen, which follows the seasons and is used in Feng Shui compass work.

## Reading a hexagram

A traditional reading has several layers:

1. **Judgment (卦辞)** — the overall situation. Example: Hexagram 1 乾 Qián: 「元亨利贞」 ("Sublime success; perseverance furthers").
2. **Image (象)** — a natural picture and the conduct it suggests. For Qián: 「天行健，君子以自强不息」 ("Heaven moves with strength; the noble person strengthens themselves without ceasing").
3. **Line statements (爻辞)** — advice for a specific position. Lines that "move" (老阴 / 老阳) indicate change into a second hexagram.
4. **Position and correspondence** — odd lines (1, 3, 5) are yang places, even lines (2, 4, 6) are yin places; a line "in its correct place" is traditionally favourable, and lines 1–4, 2–5, 3–6 may "correspond".

## Hexagram 3 屯 Zhūn (Difficulty at the Beginning) — an example

Hexagram 3 shows Water (☵) above Thunder (☳). The judgment reads 「元亨，利贞。勿用有攸往，利建侯」: "Sublime success through perseverance; do not undertake a journey; it furthers one to appoint helpers." Traditionally this hexagram describes the confusion of a new beginning, like a sprout pushing through soil. The counsel is to organise, find allies and not force progress. Modern readers often apply this to starting a business or project: the emphasis is on preparation and building support rather than on a prediction of success or failure.

## Divination methods

The classical method uses fifty yarrow stalks (蓍草) and a counting procedure recorded in the Great Commentary. The later three-coin method (掷钱法), popular since the Han dynasty, assigns values 2 (tails) and 3 (heads); sums of 6, 7, 8 and 9 produce old yin, young yang, young yin and old yang lines respectively. Both methods build the hexagram from the bottom line upward.

## How to treat the I Ching today

- **Historical/classical teaching:** a Zhou-era divination manual that became a philosophical classic.
- **Traditional belief:** a way to consult the pattern of the moment before acting.
- **Modern interpretation:** a structured framework for reflection, decision analysis and self-examination; Carl Jung described it in terms of "synchronicity".
- **Evidence-based view:** there is no scientific evidence that the procedure predicts external events; its value lies in perspective and disciplined reflection.

Traditional readings should never replace qualified financial, legal or medical advice.
